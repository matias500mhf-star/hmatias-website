How to Prepare a Supply Request for Quotation for Businesses in Angola

A clear request for quotation does more than collect prices. It helps ensure that suppliers are responding to the same requirement, under the same conditions, with enough information to support a responsible commercial decision.

8 November 2026Estimated reading time: 7–9 minutesHMATIAS Editorial Team

What a decision-ready RFQ should make clear

A supplier should be able to identify exactly what is required, how much, where and when it must be delivered, which documents are needed, and how the full commercial cost will be assessed. If any of these points is missing, mark it as an open question instead of silently assuming an answer.

In this guide

  1. Describe the requirement precisely
  2. State quantities and units of measure
  3. Define delivery location and conditions
  4. Request the right supporting documents
  5. Separate price, taxes, transport and payment terms
  6. Compare quotations on an equivalent basis
  7. Confirm availability before making commitments

When a business asks for prices without defining exactly what it intends to buy, the replies may look comparable while hiding important differences: materials with different characteristics, different units of measure, excluded transport or delivery dates that do not meet the actual requirement.

This guide provides a simple structure for requests for quotations for goods and materials. It is operational guidance for commercial enquiries. For public tenders or processes governed by specific rules, the procedure documents and applicable framework take precedence.

1. Describe the requirement precisely

Start with the product or material name, but do not rely on the commercial name alone. Add the reference, model, standard or technical feature needed to identify the requested item.

  • Material, composition or raw material, where relevant.
  • Dimensions, capacity, power, grade or required performance.
  • Brand and model if mandatory; otherwise state whether technically compatible equivalents are acceptable.
  • Standards, certificates, technical data sheets or samples, where required.
  • Condition: new, refurbished or another expressly accepted condition.

If some technical parameters are unknown, identify what is mandatory and what the supplier may propose. It is better to flag a gap and request clarification than to invent a specification.

2. State quantities and units of measure

Give both the quantity and its unit: item, pair, box, pack, metre, kilogram, litre or another suitable unit. If the commercial pack contains several units, state the pack configuration as well.

For example, instead of writing “gloves — 500”, specify “500 pairs of gloves, size allocation to be confirmed, individually packed or supplied in boxes of X pairs”. If the size allocation is not final, mark it as pending information.

3. Define delivery location and conditions

The cost of a supply can change significantly depending on location, access, unloading and urgency. Include:

  • Province, municipality and delivery address or point.
  • Required date or latest acceptable delivery deadline.
  • Whether transport, unloading, assembly or installation must be included.
  • Access restrictions, receiving hours and operational contact, where relevant.
  • Whether delivery is to be made in one shipment or in stages.

If the exact location cannot be shared at the first enquiry stage, provide the area and explain that logistics costs will be confirmed once the destination is validated.

4. Request the right supporting documents

Documents depend on the product, risk and buyer requirements. A technical data sheet, catalogue, certificate, declaration of conformity, warranty, actual product photograph or sample may be relevant. Suppliers may also be asked for corporate and tax identification documents when needed for assessment and contracting.

Avoid requesting documents without a clear purpose. Explain what is required to assess the offer and what will only be needed before award or delivery.

5. Separate price, taxes, transport and payment terms

Ask suppliers to show commercial elements separately. This makes it easier to understand what is included and avoids comparing a delivered price with a price that only covers the goods at the supplier’s premises.

  • Unit price and total price, with currency stated.
  • Applicable taxes or charges, shown separately where possible.
  • Transport, insurance, unloading and other additional costs.
  • Quotation validity period.
  • Lead time and stated availability.
  • Payment terms and any advance payment.
  • Warranty, support and replacement policy, where relevant.

Ask the supplier to expressly identify every assumption, exclusion or alternative included in the price.

A practical RFQ structure you can reuse

Use this as a starting point and adapt it to the product, risk and procurement process. Delete fields that do not apply and mark unknown details as pending.

REQUEST FOR QUOTATION — [REFERENCE]
Buyer / company: [name]
Item and technical specification: [exact description]
Quantity and unit: [quantity + unit]
Acceptable equivalents: [yes/no + criteria]
Required documents: [list]
Delivery point: [province, municipality, location]
Required delivery date: [date / lead time]
Price requested: [unit and total, currency]
Taxes and logistics: [show separately]
Payment terms: [requested terms]
Quotation validity: [number of days]
Supplier clarifications / exclusions: [list]
Contact and submission deadline: [details]

6. Compare quotations on an equivalent basis

Before selecting an offer, check that each quotation responds to the same specification, quantity, location and deadline. A lower price may exclude transport, offer a different technical feature or include a delivery time that does not work.

A comparison sheet can include:

  • Technical compliance with the request.
  • Comparable total price, including known additional costs.
  • Delivery time and confirmed capacity.
  • Required documents and warranties.
  • Payment terms and quotation validity.
  • Risks, dependencies and outstanding clarifications.

7. Confirm availability before making commitments

Receiving a quotation does not, by itself, prove that the item is still available, that the price remains valid or that the delivery date can be met. Before issuing a purchase order or making a commitment to your own customer, confirm availability, price validity, lead time, quantity and delivery conditions in writing.

For imports or cross-border supply, also clarify origin, dispatch point, known logistics costs, transport documents and who is responsible for each stage. Do not treat preliminary estimates as final costs.

Warning signs in a quotation

Be cautious when the supplier does not identify the exact model or specification, gives a price without currency or validity, excludes delivery without saying so, promises a date without confirming capacity, or asks for an advance payment without clear written terms. These are reasons to clarify and verify—not, by themselves, proof of misconduct.

Checklist before sending the RFQ

  • Product or service identified with sufficient specification.
  • Quantity and unit of measure defined.
  • Acceptable alternatives clearly stated.
  • Delivery location, deadline and conditions described.
  • Mandatory supporting documents identified.
  • Price, taxes, transport and payment terms requested separately.
  • Quotation validity period requested.
  • Comparison criteria defined.
