A clear request reduces questions, prevents quotations based on different products and helps confirm availability, price, lead time and logistics before a commitment is made.

A quotation is only useful when the supplier understands exactly what is being requested. A product name on its own is rarely enough. Model, material, dimensions, standard, quantity, unit and delivery location can affect the price and determine whether two proposals are genuinely comparable.

## 1. Identify the product precisely

Start with the product's commercial name and add a reference, brand, model or code where available. For technical materials, include the features that determine compatibility: composition, dimensions, thickness, capacity, grade, standard or finish.

When there is no exact reference, describe the intended use or required performance. Do not make a preference a mandatory specification unless necessary; also state whether equivalent alternatives may be considered.

## 2. Specify quantity and unit of supply

Record the quantity and its unit: piece, pair, box, roll, metre, square metre, kilogram or litre. Where packaging affects the purchase, indicate the number of units per box or pack.

Avoid descriptions such as “a few boxes” or “standard quantity”. If the final quantity is still being assessed, provide an estimate and mark it as such.

## 3. State the delivery destination and conditions

A municipality or province alone may not be enough to assess logistics. Where possible, identify the delivery location, access conditions, unloading requirements and any restrictions on delivery times or site entry.

Clarify whether you expect the price to include transport or whether logistics should be quoted separately. This avoids comparing a supplier collection price with another price that already includes delivery.

## 4. Define the required timing

Provide a target date or delivery window. If there is a firm deadline, identify it clearly. If timing is flexible, say so; this may allow the supplier to consult more sources or identify a suitable logistics option.

## 5. List documents and compliance requirements

For equipment, regulated materials or institutional supply, identify the documents required in advance: technical datasheet, certificate, declaration of conformity, warranty, manual, sample or other specific evidence.

State whether brand or origin is mandatory. If it is only a preference, make that distinction clear.

## A practical request template

| Field | Information to include |
|---|---|
| Product | Description and reference, where available |
| Specification | Material, dimensions, model, standard or essential features |
| Quantity | Amount and unit of supply |
| Alternatives | Whether equivalent products are acceptable and on what basis |
| Delivery | Destination, unloading conditions and transport requirements |
| Timing | Required date or delivery window |
| Documentation | Certificates, datasheets, warranty or other requirements |

## 6. Compare proposals on the same basis

When quotations arrive, confirm that each one covers the same specification and quantity. Compare prices on the same basis: unit, packaging, currency, taxes, transport, quotation validity and payment terms.

A lower quoted price may not represent the lowest final cost if it relates to a different specification, an unsuitable lead time or logistics charged separately.

## How HMATIAS handles a supply request

HMATIAS works on a request basis and does not present its own stock as guaranteed availability. After receiving a request, the team reviews the specifications, consults suppliers and seeks to confirm price, availability, origin, lead time and logistics. Only after these checks can a proposal be prepared for the client's review.

Incomplete requests do not need to be abandoned. The team may ask for missing information before proceeding. The important point is to distinguish confirmed information from estimates and preferences.
