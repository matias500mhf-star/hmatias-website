Decide beyond price: how to assess a supply quotation

The lowest quotation does not always represent the best decision. A sound comparison considers what the price includes, whether the product meets the specification, and which delivery and payment commitments are actually confirmed.

9 November 20265–7 minute readHMATIAS Editorial Team

The central point

A useful commercial comparison puts every quotation on the same basis: specification, quantity, known total cost, delivery location and timing, payment terms, warranty and confirmed availability. Unit price is only one variable.

When two quotations show different amounts, the first question should not simply be “which is cheaper?”. It should be “are we comparing the same item under the same conditions?”. A price difference may come from a different specification, excluded logistics costs or commercial terms that transfer more risk to the buyer.

This Insight is general management guidance for private commercial enquiries. For public tenders or procedures governed by specific rules, the relevant tender documents and applicable requirements take precedence.

1. Create a comparable basis

Before reviewing prices, confirm that every supplier has answered the same requirement. Similar commercial product names can conceal material differences in model, dimensions, capacity, quality, certification, composition or performance.

  • Check technical specifications and accepted equivalents.
  • Normalise quantities and units of measure.
  • Identify everything included and excluded.
  • Mark incomplete offers as pending instead of assuming compliance.

A quotation that fails a mandatory requirement should not be treated as equivalent simply because it carries a lower price.

2. Calculate known total cost

The cost relevant to a decision may include more than the item price. Depending on the supply, check separately:

  • Unit price, quantity and currency.
  • Taxes and charges identified by the supplier.
  • Transport, insurance, unloading, assembly or installation.
  • Packaging, accessories or necessary consumables.
  • Known maintenance or support costs, where relevant.

Do not convert estimates into final figures. Where a cost is not yet confirmed, record it as pending and identify who must validate it. Two quotations are financially comparable only when their assumptions and exclusions are visible.

3. Validate delivery and availability

A date shown in a quotation may depend on stock, production, import, transport or payment confirmation. Ask the supplier to distinguish what is available, what must still be sourced and what remains subject to confirmation.

Before making a commitment to your client, obtain written confirmation of quantity, price validity, timing, delivery location and any conditions that may change the date. Receiving a quotation does not, by itself, guarantee reserved stock or future availability.

4. Review terms and risk

Payment conditions, quotation validity, warranty and the handling of defects or replacements affect the buyer’s commercial exposure. Check in particular:

  • Whether any deposit is linked to defined milestones and evidence.
  • Whether delivery dates and the consequences of delay are clear.
  • Whether the warranty covers the product and intended use.
  • Whether exclusions, dependencies or price changes are specified.
  • Whether required documents will be supplied before or with delivery.

Do not assume a condition is safe simply because it appears in a quotation. Clarify material points and obtain written confirmation before approving an order.

5. Record the decision

A defensible decision should make it possible to understand why a quotation was selected. Record the criteria used, the evidence reviewed, pending points and accepted exceptions. When the lowest price is not the best option, document the compliance, cost, timing or risk difference that supports the choice.

Practical rule

Do not compare an ex-supplier price with a delivered price; do not treat a supplier’s availability statement as reserved stock; and do not assume a product is equivalent while its specification remains unconfirmed.
